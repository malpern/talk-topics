A PDF → EPUB pipeline

Six Hundred Pages,
Four-Inch Screen

MIT's 6.01 textbook is 320 pages of prose, Python, circuit diagrams, and equations. I wanted to read it on a 4-inch e-reader. Here's everything that broke on the way to a clean EPUB.

A project by Micah Alpern

I have a 4-inch e-paper reader — an 800×480 grayscale panel about the size of a deck of cards. It is a wonderful object for reading prose and a hostile environment for everything else. PDFs are the worst case: a 320-page technical textbook laid out for US Letter, rendered onto a screen a fifth the width, is a pinch-zoom nightmare.

The book I wanted on it was MIT 6.01SC — Introduction to EECS I, the OpenCourseWare textbook. The goal was a reflowable EPUB: text that rewraps to the screen, fonts the reader can resize, a real table of contents, and — the hard part — the equations, code, and circuit diagrams surviving the trip. Visual fidelity was negotiable. The reading experience was not.

Section one

The four-inch problem

The goal
A reflowable EPUB — not a shrunk-down PDF — that keeps chapters, equations, figures, and code, and throws away page furniture.

A PDF is a description of ink on a fixed page. An EPUB is a description of a document — headings, paragraphs, figures — that a reader lays out however it likes. Converting one to the other is really an act of re-interpretation: you have to recover the structure the PDF threw away when it froze everything into coordinates.

For a novel that's easy; it's all one column of prose. For a technical textbook it's genuinely hard. The 6.01 book has section numbering four levels deep, hundreds of Python listings, 200-plus circuit and state-machine diagrams, and equations on nearly every page of the signals chapter. Each of those is a different failure mode. And running across the top of every page is the same banner — 6.01 — Spring 2011 — April 25, 2011 and a page number — that I needed to not end up sprinkled through the reading text.

So the requirements list wrote itself: reflowable EPUB3, preserved heading hierarchy, a hierarchical TOC, figures kept with their captions, equations as real math (not screenshots), code that wraps instead of running off the screen, grayscale images sized for 480 pixels, resizable fonts, and every scrap of page furniture removed. Then: prove it's all there.

Section two

Picking a brain

The decision
For a textbook, the extractor lives or dies on two things: equations and figures. That picks the tool.

The naive approach is pdftotext. It is fast, it is everywhere, and on this book it produces garbage: equations flatten into word-soup, figures vanish, and two-column reasoning interleaves into nonsense. The text layer is there, but the structure isn't.

The modern approach is a machine-learning document parser. Two lead the field: Docling (IBM) and Marker (built on the Surya OCR models). Both detect layout, reading order, tables, and math with neural models rather than heuristics. I chose Marker for one reason that matters enormously here: it emits equations as LaTeX and extracts every figure as its own image file, with reading order intact. For a book that is one-third math and diagrams, that's the whole game. The cost — and it is real — is speed: Marker runs vision models over every page.

The downstream half of the pipeline was never in doubt. pandoc turns Markdown into a standards-clean EPUB3, converts LaTeX math into MathML (which scales with the reader's font instead of being a fixed-size picture), builds the navigation TOC, and can split the book into one file per chapter. And epubcheck, the reference validator, would be the judge at the end. The shape of the pipeline: Marker → Markdown → repair → pandoc → epubcheck.

Section three

The Mac that wouldn't load SciPy

The yak
Before extracting a single page, the ML stack refused to even import. A loader bug from the future.

Marker was already installed. It crashed on import — not in its own code, but deep inside SciPy, pulled in transitively by the transformers library:

ImportError: dlopen(.../scipy/sparse/linalg/_propack/_spropack...so):
  tried: '..._spropack...so' (section '__DATA/__thread_bss' has a
  zero-fill section type, but offset field is not zero)

That error is the macOS dynamic linker rejecting a Mach-O binary it considers malformed. The prebuilt SciPy wheel for Python 3.10 was compiled with an older toolchain; the newer, stricter dyld on this machine won't load it. Upgrading SciPy didn't help — every 3.10 wheel hit the same wall. The problem wasn't the version, it was the interpreter generation: the 3.10 wheels predate the toolchain the OS now insists on.

The fix was to stop fighting the wheels and move up a Python. A fresh 3.12 virtual environment pulls 3.12-era wheels, built with the toolchain the loader expects:

python3.12 -m venv .venv
source .venv/bin/activate
pip install marker-pdf      # 3.12 wheels load cleanly

SciPy imported on the first try. It's a good reminder that "it doesn't import" is sometimes a packaging-and-platform problem wearing a Python-error costume — and that the cheapest fix is often a newer interpreter, not a night spent compiling from source.

Section four

Ninety-eight minutes

The grind
Layout, then OCR across 3,474 text regions, then equations, then tables. On a laptop GPU, it takes a while.

Marker runs in stages, and you watch them tick by: recognize layout on all 320 pages, then run text recognition across every detected region, then the equation model, then tables. On Apple-Silicon GPU acceleration (TORCH_DEVICE=mps) the whole book took 98 minutes — most of it the OCR pass over 3,474 individual text regions at roughly one per second.

What came out the other side was genuinely good. The figures extracted as clean grayscale line art — here's a finite-state-machine diagram and a circuit, straight from the pipeline, untouched:

And the equations arrived as LaTeX, ready for pandoc to turn into MathML. By the numbers: the cleaned text held 97% of the words in the PDF's own text layer — the missing 3% being exactly the running headers, page numbers, and the PDF's own table of contents that I wanted gone. 1,030 equations, 217 figures, 460 code blocks. A strong starting point — which is not the same as a finished one. The interesting bugs were hiding in the math.

Section five

The R that thought it was a real number

The detective story
One mis-read character, repeated 322 times, silently rewrote the math of an entire chapter.

The Signals and Systems chapter is built on a single operator: R, the delay (right-shift) operator. RX is the signal X delayed one step; R²X delays it two. Whole pages are algebra in R.

Scanning the extracted math, I noticed something off: the operator had been read not as the letter R but as \Re — the LaTeX command for the real part of a complex number, which renders as a fancy ℜ. The OCR model saw a stylized capital R and reached for the closest symbol it knew. It did this 322 times. Every delay equation in the chapter was quietly wrong.

Before mass-editing a textbook's math, you verify the assumption. Could any of those be a genuine real-part operator? I read the chapter's complex-number sections: they use j for the imaginary unit and spell out "real part" in words. The real-part symbol is never used. Every single \Re was the delay operator. So the fix was safe, and surgical:

# Surya read the delay operator R as \Re (real-part). The book
# never uses real-part notation, so every \Re here is the operator.
seg = re.sub(r"\\Re(?![a-zA-Z])", r"\\mathrm{R}", seg)

That one substitution corrected more equations than any other change in the project. It's also the part of the job a fully automated tool would have shipped wrong: the pipeline can't know that this book never means "real part." Catching it took reading the prose around the math — exactly the judgment call worth being a human for.

Section six

The footnote that broke the book

The cascade
A garbled footnote marker produced fatal XHTML errors — and 120 phantom errors downstream. One decision fixed all of them.

The first build produced EPUBs, and epubcheck lit up red. The fatal one:

FATAL(RSC-016): The element type "p" must be terminated
  by the matching end-tag "</p>".

The culprit was footnote markers. The OCR had rendered a superscript footnote number as broken inline HTML — literally <sup>&</sup>lt;sup>35</sup> — with an unbalanced closing tag. pandoc passed the raw HTML straight through, the stray </sup> broke the paragraph, and the whole chapter file became invalid XHTML. Worse, that corruption rippled: 120 more errors about "fragment identifier not defined" in the navigation, because the broken markup had mangled the heading IDs the TOC pointed at.

I could have patched each bad footnote. The better fix was to change the contract. Those <sup> tags were the only HTML I actually wanted; everything else that looked like a tag was either OCR noise or, in this book, literal BNF grammar like <statement> and <booleanExpr> that should display as angle-bracket text. So I converted the real super/subscripts to native Markdown and told pandoc to stop trusting raw HTML entirely:

# real footnotes → native Markdown super/subscript
<sup>35</sup>   →   ^35^
# then build with raw HTML disabled, so every stray
# <tag> becomes harmless escaped text:
pandoc --from=markdown-raw_html+tex_math_dollars+superscript+subscript ...

That single flag fixed the fatal errors and the 120 phantom TOC errors at once — because the navigation IDs were only broken as a side effect of the bad markup. The grammar placeholders now render exactly as the textbook intends, and the math still becomes MathML, because tex_math_dollars is independent of HTML parsing. Final score: nine EPUBs, zero errors, zero warnings.

Section seven

Built to re-run

The finish
A one-command pipeline, a quality scorecard, and an honest list of what still needs a human.

Every fix lives in a script, not in a hand-edited file, so the whole thing reruns from the source PDF with one command. The pipeline stores a hash of the PDF and only re-runs the 98-minute extraction if the source actually changed; otherwise it jumps straight to cleanup and build. Page furniture stripped, headings rebuilt by section number, captions reattached to figures, images flattened to grayscale and capped at 760px, equations to MathML, then validated.

And the honest caveats, because a conversion report that claims perfection is lying: two equations out of 1,030 are malformed in the OCR and fall back to raw TeX — one probability matrix in the estimation chapter, one broken footnote superscript. The complex numbers in the signals chapter render in a fraktur font instead of italic — mathematically correct, stylistically quirky. And a handful of unnumbered sub-headings sit one level too high because Marker had no section number to infer depth from. For daily reading, none of these are blockers; they're a short, specific punch-list rather than a vague "needs review."

One thing I'd still verify on the actual hardware: the equations are MathML, which the best e-readers render beautifully and the worst ignore. If a given device can't, the same Markdown can regenerate the book with equations as small images instead. That's the quiet advantage of keeping the whole thing as a scripted pipeline over a pile of manual edits — the book isn't a one-off artifact, it's a build target. Change the source, run one command, get a fresh EPUB.
