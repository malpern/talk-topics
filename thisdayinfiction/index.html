Dates That Don't Exist

Fictional dates break your date library, resist your metadata APIs, and require a crowd to get right. We built a Django app to wrangle them anyway.

A project by Peter Theobald (@ControlAltPete)

November 5, 1955. You know what happened. You probably know it better than you know what happened on most actual dates in the 1950s. Marty McFly arrived in Hill Valley, and that date became permanently etched in the minds of anyone who grew up with a VHS player.

Fiction does this. It colonizes the calendar. April 4 belongs to Winston Smith. October 21, 2015 belongs to the hoverboard. March 18, 2263 belongs to Korben Dallas and the Fifth Element. These dates feel more real than most history, because they come attached to stories we care about. We built a web app to collect them all in one place and answer a single question every morning: what happened in fiction today?

Section one

November fifth

NOV 5, 1955 Back to the Future
The idea
Some dates belong to fiction. We built a database to keep track.

There's a particular thrill when you notice the date. You're watching a movie, and a character writes something on a chalkboard, or a timestamp flashes on screen, and suddenly the fiction is anchored to the same calendar you use. April 26, 1986 -- the night of the prom in Donnie Darko. January 12, 1997 -- HAL 9000 becomes operational in 2001: A Space Odyssey. May 12, 1984 -- Kyle Reese arrives from the future in The Terminator.

These aren't random. Filmmakers and novelists choose specific dates with intention. Sometimes they're plot points. Sometimes they're Easter eggs. Sometimes they're just the kind of detail that makes a fictional world feel lived-in. And fans remember them -- often more precisely than they remember actual historical events.

ThisDayInFiction is a curated, crowd-sourced calendar of these moments. Visit on any day of the year and see what happened in fiction on that date -- across movies, TV shows, and literature. The home page always shows today. The calendar lets you browse any month. Every event links to its source work, with cover art, a description, and links to watch or buy.

The idea is simple enough that you can explain it in one sentence. The interesting part is everything that breaks when you try to build it.

Section two

The calendar

APR 4 Nineteen Eighty-Four
The twist
Fictional dates don't follow the rules of real ones.

Winston Smith begins his diary on April 4. But what year? The novel is called 1984, but Orwell deliberately obscures whether that's the actual year in the story. Fan debates aside, the honest answer is: we know the month and day, not the year.

This turns out to be the first interesting design problem. Many fictional events carry only a month and day -- diary entries from ambiguous timelines, recurring holidays in fictional universes, events from franchises where the calendar date matters more than the year. If you force every event into a standard date format, you have to invent years you don't actually have. And once you start making things up in a database of facts, you've already lost.

The solution is to store month, day, and year as three separate fields, with year being optional. April 4 with no year is just that -- month 4, day 4, year unknown. No invented years, no placeholder values.

Every event also has a second temporal coordinate: when the work itself was released. Back to the Future is set in 1955 but came out in 1985. The Fifth Element is set in 2263 but came out in 1997. These are completely different axes. "Show me everything set in the 1950s" and "show me everything released in the 1980s" are different questions, and confusing them corrupts both answers. So we track them separately: the fictional year and the release year.

It's a small decision, but it unlocks everything else. The calendar can show you what happened on any date without lying about what it doesn't know. The browse page can sort by fictional timeline or by release decade. And when two films share a title and a date -- which happens more often than you'd think -- the release year is what tells them apart.

Section three

Letting strangers in

JAN 1, 1976 Rocky
The design
Crowd-sourced data without chaos.

No one person knows every fictional date. The seed database started with 88 events -- a respectable foundation, but thin enough that most days of the year had nothing to show. The site needs contributors. And contributors need a way in that doesn't require an admin to personally approve every entry.

The standard approach is a moderation queue. Every submission waits for a human to bless it. That works until you have more submissions than admins, which for a solo project is immediately. We needed something smarter.

The solution is crowd consensus. When someone submits an event, the system checks whether other users have independently submitted the same thing -- same date, same title. If three different people say the same fictional event happened on the same day, it's probably real. Auto-approved.

When multiple submissions describe the same event, the system picks the most complete one to publish -- the one with the most metadata filled in. The others aren't rejected. They're marked as merged: your contribution helped confirm the event, even if your specific version wasn't the one that went live. Everyone gets credit.

This distinction matters for the trust system. Users start as Registered -- every submission goes through the queue. But after five approved contributions with a rejection rate under 20%, you're automatically promoted to Trusted, and your submissions bypass the queue entirely. Since merged submissions count as approvals, contributing to crowd consensus accelerates your path to trust. The system rewards participation without punishing redundancy.

There's a trap in the crowd logic, though, and Rocky walks right into it. Suppose a remake comes out and someone submits an event for the same date. The original 1976 film and the remake share the same title, the same calendar date -- the system sees three submissions and wants to auto-approve. But they're different movies.

The guard is the release year. When pending submissions in the same group have conflicting release years, auto-approval is blocked. The group sits in the admin queue until a human looks at it. A false hold is always better than a false publication.

Section four

Finding the poster

MAR 18, 2263 The Fifth Element
The detective work
Matching titles to cover art across three APIs that don't agree on anything.

A calendar of fictional events without cover art is just a spreadsheet. The poster is what makes you stop scrolling. But 88 seed events loaded from a text file had no images, no creator names, no release years. Just titles and dates. Every event needed to be matched against external databases to fill in the gaps.

For movies and TV, TMDB is excellent. Search a title, optionally filter by year, get back poster art and structured metadata. It handles remakes, distinguishes between the 1976 and 2018 versions of a film, and almost always returns the right thing.

Books are where it gets messy. Google Books has no way to filter by publication year. If you search for "The Time Machine," you get dozens of editions spanning a century -- and the API returns the edition date, not the original publication date. A 2023 reprint of H.G. Wells returns 2023, not 1895. To find the true first-publication year, we fetch far more results than we need, use fuzzy string matching to confirm the titles actually match our query, and take the earliest year across all the confirmed matches. It's a hack, but it reliably finds 1895 for Wells and 1949 for Orwell.

Then there's Sag Harbor. Colson Whitehead's novel about a teenager in the Hamptons. Search Google Books for "Sag Harbor" and you get travel guides, local histories, and real estate listings for the actual town on Long Island. The novel is buried on page two. The fix: include the author's name in the search query. "Sag Harbor" plus "Whitehead" returns the right book immediately. Without it, the algorithm confidently presents you with a road map of eastern Long Island.

When Google Books fails entirely, the system falls back to Open Library -- a community-maintained catalog run by the Internet Archive. It requires no API key, has good coverage of well-known titles, and provides its own first-publication-year field. Between the three sources -- TMDB for visual media, Google Books for literature, Open Library as a safety net -- most events end up with a cover image, a creator, and a release year. The rest get fixed by hand in the admin.

Section five

What day is it?

MAY 12, 1984 The Terminator
The edge case
"Today" depends on where you're standing.

The very first time someone tested the site, the home page showed the wrong date. The server was in UTC. The user was in California. At 5pm on April 3rd in San Francisco, the page was cheerfully displaying events for April 4th. For a site whose entire reason to exist is "what happened in fiction today," there is no more fundamental bug.

The fix is a small piece of JavaScript that reads the browser's timezone and stores it in a cookie. The server reads the cookie on every request and adjusts accordingly. A user in Tokyo and a user in New York see different events at midnight, and both see the right ones.

The first visit is the awkward case -- no cookie yet, so the server guesses UTC, the JavaScript corrects it, and the page reloads once. After that, the cookie persists and every subsequent visit renders correctly with no flash.

Unless cookies are blocked. Then the JavaScript sets the cookie, reloads, finds no cookie, reloads again -- forever. An infinite loop, triggered by exactly the kind of privacy-conscious user you'd want to keep on your site. The fix is a single flag in the browser's session storage: "I already tried to reload once, don't do it again." One line of defense between a working website and a browser tab that spins until the heat death of the universe.

Section six

The birthday hook

OCT 21, 2015 Back to the Future Part II
What's next
Making it personal.

The calendar is useful. But "what happened in fiction on your birthday" is personal. Enter your birth date and the app gives you your own permanent page of fictional events -- the same data, reframed around a date you'll never forget. It's the difference between a reference tool and something you share with your friends.

The Works page takes the data in the other direction. Instead of "what happened on this date," it answers "what dates appear in this work." Every film and book gets its own page listing all its fictional dates in chronological order. Back to the Future alone spans four dates across 130 years -- 1885, 1955, 1985, 2015 -- all anchored to one fictional family. Seeing the full timeline laid out is more interesting than any single entry.

A public JSON API was designed from day one. Every event is available as structured data -- dates, metadata IDs, cover images, media types. The API exists because the database has value beyond a single web interface. Laura E. Hall's "Dating the Movies" is the most obvious parallel -- a carefully researched collection of film dates, maintained by hand as editorial content. It's the same intellectual project in a different form. A clean API makes collaboration possible without either side changing how they work.

What the project needs now is density. Enough events on every date that the home page always delivers something surprising, whether you visit in January or July, whether you care about science fiction or period dramas. The database grows every time someone remembers a date from a movie they love. The crowd-sourcing handles scale. The trust system handles quality. The rest is just time, and contributors who care enough to add one more entry to the calendar of things that never happened.
