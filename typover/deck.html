The Typo That Refused to Stay Fixed

The difficult part of autocorrect is not spotting teh. It is changing exactly three characters without disturbing everything around them.

Micah Alpern · Hacker Dojo

The dangerous shortcut

Read the whole field. Fix one word. Write the whole field back.

It works in a demo—and quietly moves the cursor, erases rich text, breaks undo, or races the next keystroke.

A smaller contract

Change less.

01 · detectWait for a word boundary, then inspect the token that just ended.No speculative rewrite while the user is still composing.
02 · replaceTarget the smallest exact accessibility range.Preserve surrounding text, selection, and the user’s momentum.
03 · revealMark the correction subtly and make “Change Back” immediate.Automation should be visible, reversible, and quiet.

The interaction, not a mock product UI

A correction should feel like a reversible edit.

Agency

Change Back

The replacement retains enough provenance to undo itself—not the user’s next thought.

Why this gets hard

Text fields are not one thing.

SurfaceRiskDesign response
Plain native fieldsSelection driftExact-range replacement and cursor verification
Rich editorsFormatting lossNever rewrite the entire document value
Browser/Electron editorsAccessibility behavior variesDetect capability; fail small and visibly
Rapid typingCorrection races new inputRevalidate target and surrounding context

A system responsibility

The cursor belongs to the writer.

The app’s strongest behavior is often restraint: if the range is ambiguous, the field is protected, or the user has moved on, do nothing.

Takeaway

Good autocorrect does not take over the sentence. It borrows three characters—and gives them back on request.
